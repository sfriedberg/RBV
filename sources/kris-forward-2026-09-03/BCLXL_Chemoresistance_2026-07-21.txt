One Sentence Summary: Using BH3 Profiling as well as small molecule and CRISPR
screening across hundreds of cell lines, we find that solid tumors broadly co-depend on BCL-XL
and MCL-1 but evade targeting of pro-survival proteins due to HSP27-driven rapid apoptotic
adaptation, whereas mitotic arrest–inducing therapies indirectly suppress MCL-1 and create a
BCL-XL–dependent apoptotic vulnerability that can be safely exploited with the platelet-sparing
BCL-XL PROTAC DT2216 to overcome chemoresistance.
Abstract:
Despite therapeutic advances, intrinsic and acquired chemoresistance remain major barriers to durable
cancer responses. Because most cytotoxic, targeted, and immune-based therapies induce apoptosis,
therapy resistance is frequently driven by upregulation of pro-survival BCL-2 family proteins. Using BH3
profiling, CRISPR, and small-molecule screening across hundreds of cancer cell lines and primary
specimens, we find that nearly all solid tumors depend on both BCL-XL and MCL-1 for survival.
However, we find that inhibiting either protein alone produces only modest cell death due to rapid
apoptotic adaptation (RAA) – a compensatory upregulation of the targeted and alternative pro-survival
proteins driven by HSP27 activation. HSP27 inhibition suppresses RAA and enhances BH3 mimetic-
induced apoptosis. Further, although dual BCL-XL/MCL-1 targeting triggers synergistic apoptosis across
solid tumors, including chemoresistant TP53-mutant cancers, this approach is not tolerable in vivo. To
identify safer strategies, we screened for therapies that indirectly modulate these dependencies and found
that paclitaxel-induced mitotic arrest selectively depletes MCL-1 while upregulating BCL-XL, driving a
targetable apoptotic vulnerability due to BCL-XL sequestration of pro-apoptotic BIM, BAX, and BAK.
This vulnerability is also induced by Aurora A kinase and PLK1 inhibitors and microtubule-targeting
antibody-drug conjugate payloads, rendering chemoresistant tumors highly sensitive to BCL-XL targeting.
In chemoresistant ovarian cancer xenograft and PDX models, paclitaxel combined with the platelet-
sparing BCL-XL PROTAC DT2216 safely eradicates tumors. DT2216 also reduces RAA, demonstrating
that targeted degradation of pro-survival proteins is superior to conventional inhibition. These findings
provide a mechanistic and translational framework for safely co-targeting MCL-1 and BCL-XL to
overcome chemoresistance in solid tumors.
Main Text:
INTRODUCTION
Inherent or acquired treatment resistance continues to be a significant barrier to long-term remission and
cure across both blood cancers and solid tumors(1). Most anti-cancer agents including cytotoxic and
targeted therapies act by interrupting essential signaling pathways or causing damage to universal cellular
structures like DNA and microtubules, generating stress that activates pro-apoptotic proteins and triggers
apoptosis in sensitive cancer cells(2). Clinical outcomes, however, vary widely, mostly due to differences
in tumor responses to therapy and the development of chemoresistance. Currently, 80-90% of mortality in
cancer patients is either directly or indirectly attributed to chemoresistance(3). Resistance can be
restricted to a specific drug, or different drugs with varying modes of action, which is referred to as
multidrug resistance (MDR)(4). Since most cancer therapies produce pro-apoptotic signaling in cancer
cells, the suppression of apoptotic cell death is a major mechanism by which cancer cells can evade the
cell death that should be triggered by these agents. Indeed, cancer cells frequently develop
chemoresistance by upregulating pro-survival proteins or downregulating pro-apoptotic effectors,
allowing them to evade cell death and continue proliferating despite exposure to cytotoxic agents(5).
Based on this, targeting the apoptotic machinery, for example by inhibiting BCL-2 family proteins or
mimicking the action of pro-apoptotic factors, offers a promising approach to overcoming
chemoresistance(6).

Ovarian cancers (OvCa) of epithelial origin comprise a heterogeneous group of malignancies that are
typically diagnosed at an advanced stage and have low cure rates(7). The most common and clinically
intractable of these are high-grade serous ovarian carcinomas (HGSOC), which exhibit an alarmingly low
5-year overall survival (OS) rate of 35-48%(8, 9). This OS rate has not meaningfully improved in the past
20 years due to a lack of clinically actionable targets, as well as the profound genomic instability that is a
hallmark of this disease. This instability is driven by near-ubiquitous mutations in TP53 as well as
deficiencies in homologous recombination and other DNA repair pathways in a significant percentage of
HGSOC cases(8, 10) – these features, which are also common in other solid cancers(11, 12), contribute to
the development of chemoresistance if the tumor is not eradicated upon initial treatment. Treatment with
standard therapy, which includes carboplatin and paclitaxel, most commonly results in a period of
HGSOC remission of variable length, typically 6 months to several years(1, 13, 14). In 60-80% of
patients, this period is followed by tumor recurrence, with each subsequent recurrent tumor exhibiting
increased resistance to chemotherapy and almost no possibility of cure(1, 14). These high rates of relapse
and mortality demonstrate the urgent need to develop treatments that can more thoroughly eradicate
HGSOC cells to prevent the development of therapy resistance and increase OS rates. Notably,
carboplatin and paclitaxel target ubiquitous cell features, including DNA and microtubules, respectively,
and typically kill OvCa cells via the apoptosis pathway(15–17).

Apoptosis is a regulated form of cell death that is essential for normal human development as well as for
the removal of damaged or superfluous cells(5, 18–20). The mitochondrial apoptosis pathway is triggered
when a pro-apoptotic protein (BAX or BAK) is activated by a BH3-only protein (BIM or BID) to trigger
mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization (MOMP) and consequent release of cytochrome c from
mitochondria to activate caspases(20–22). Pro-survival proteins in this family (predominantly BCL-2,
BCL-XL, and MCL-1) can bind and block the activity of either the pro-apoptotic, pore-forming proteins
(BAX or BAK) or pro-apoptotic BH3-only activators (BIM, BID, PUMA). For apoptosis to occur, the
pro-survival proteins must be overwhelmed and BAX/BAK activated.

Importantly, we have previously reported that the state of the apoptosis pathway in cancer cells is an
important determinant of chemotherapy response: tumors that are primed for apoptosis respond much
more favorably in the clinic(23–25). Furthermore, we and others have shown that upregulation of pro-
survival BCL-XL and/or MCL-1(26–29) or loss of pro-apoptotic BAX and BAK(24, 30, 31) are major
drivers of therapy resistance. These findings, along with those of others(29, 32, 33) suggest that patient
outcomes can be meaningfully improved by targeting the apoptosis pathway to enhance initial
chemotherapy responses. BH3 mimetics are recently-developed agents that selectively inhibit specific
pro-survival proteins including BCL-2 (e.g., ABT-199 [venetoclax]), BCL-XL (e.g., A1331852), or MCL-
1 (e.g., S63845) or multiple proteins such as BCL-2/BCL-XL/BCL-W (e.g., ABT-263 [navitoclax]).
Among these inhibitors, only the BCL-2 inhibitor ABT-199 (venetoclax) has been successful in the clinic
for targeting apoptotic dependencies in hematologic malignancies and approved by the FDA as a
treatment for chronic lymphocytic leukemia and acute myeloid leukemia(34). The clinical development of
inhibitors targeting other BCL-2 family pro-survival proteins has stalled due to on-target and dose-
limiting toxicities in patients. For example, inhibitors of BCL-XL are known to be toxic to platelets due to
their dependence on BCL-XL for survival(35). This causes dose-limiting thrombocytopenia and increases
risk of bleeding, which has impeded the clinical development of these agents(36). To overcome this,
DT2216, a proteolysis-targeting chimera (PROTAC) has been developed that degrades BCL-XL by
targeting it to the Von Hippel-Lindau (VHL) E3 ligase for degradation instead of simply inhibiting it –
this is an important modification as platelets express minimal levels of VHL and therefore don’t degrade
BCL-XL in a manner similar to most nucleated cells(37). This prevents the loss of platelets and increases
the safety of this novel agent(37, 38), which is currently being evaluated in phase I/II clinical trials
(clinicaltrials.gov ID NCT04886622, NCT06964009, NCT06620302).

Although there have been promising recent advancements in clinical deployment of these BH3 mimetics,
it is unknown what apoptotic vulnerabilities exist in HGSOC and other solid cancers and how they may
be effectively and safely targeted with single agent inhibitors or in combinations. Herein, we report that
BCL-XL dependence is consistently detected in solid cancer cell lines, yet single target inhibition is only
mildly effective due to rapid apoptotic adaptation, which can be overcome by co-targeting other pro-
survival proteins such as the indirect targeting of MCL-1 with paclitaxel. Using this treatment strategy
primes cancer cells for apoptosis and enhances sensitivity to BCL-XL inhibition, leading to eradication of
solid tumors in vivo while avoiding toxicities.
RESULTS

Landscape of BCL-2 family dependencies across cancers

Given that “evasion of apoptosis” is one of the hallmarks of cancer(39) and that overexpression of pro-
survival BCL-2 family proteins contributes to oncogenesis and chemoresistance, we first sought to
establish the extent to which these proteins can potentially be targeted for cancer therapy across cancer
types. RNAseq data across nearly 2,000 cancer cell lines of diverse origins confirmed that most cancers
highly express at least one pro-survival gene, with the majority expressing multiple members of this
family (Figure 1A). Several notable differences in expression were evident, including the higher
expression levels of BCL2, encoding pro-survival BCL-2, being detected in hematologic malignancies
such as B and T cell leukemias and lymphomas as well as AML and Hodkin’s lymphoma. BCL2 was less
commonly expressed in solid cancer cell lines with high expression only evident in pancreatic and lung
endocrine tumors and ocular melanoma. As expected, higher BCL2 expression was positively correlated
with increased dependence on BCL-2 based on CRISPR knockout and RNAi knockdown experiments
performed within the Broad Institute’s Cancer Dependency Map (DepMap) project(40) (Figure 1B), as
well as sensitivity to the BCL-2 inhibitor venetoclax (Figure 1C), although the extent of BCL-2
dependence across cancer cell lines was very limited.

BCL2A1, encoding A1/BFL1, was narrowly expressed, with the highest expression levels evident in
anaplastic thyroid cancer, epithelioid sarcoma, fibrosarcoma, Hodkin’s lymphoma, leiomyosarcoma,
melanoma, mixed cervical carcinoma and mucosal melanoma of the vulva/vagina (Figure 1A). CRISPR
and RNAi demonstrated only limited essentiality for this gene, with weak correlations to expression
(Figure S1A). BCL2L2, encoding BCL-W, was broadly expressed but was rarely found to be an essential
gene (Figure 1A).

BCL2L1, encoding BCL-XL, was consistently expressed among hematologic and solid cancer cell lines
(Figure 1A). Similarly to BCL2, higher expression of BCL2L1 was positively correlated with higher
dependence on this gene for survival based on CRISPR and RNAi gene targeting (Figure 1D). Finally,
while there was only a weak correlation between BCL2L1 expression and sensitivity to the BCL-XL
inhibitor AZ-3202 (Figure 1E) and no correlation with sensitivity to A1331852 (Figure 1F), a strong
correlation between CRISPR essentiality and sensitivity to BCL-XL inhibitors was detected (Figure 1G).
However, across these models, the viability of cells was more strongly affected by gene knockout via
CRISPR than knockdown via RNAi (Figure 1D) or inhibitor treatment (Figure 1E-F). This finding
suggests that treatment strategies resulting in complete BCL-XL elimination (CRISPR knockout or
PROTAC-mediated degradation) may more effectively kill cancer cells than approaches that only
partially reduce BCL-XL expression (RNAi) or activity (pharmacological inhibitors).

MCL1, encoding MCL-1, was again broadly expressed among both hematologic and solid cancer cell
lines (Figure 1A), and diverse types of cancer cell lines exhibited a strong decrease in fitness when this
gene was targeted by CRISPR or RNAi, confirming strong essentiality. We did not detect a correlation
between MCL1 expression and essentiality (Figure 1H) nor sensitivity to the MCL-1 inhibitor AZD-5991
(Figure 1I) but again detected a significant correlation between CRISPR essentiality and sensitivity to
AZD-5991 (Figure 1J) with CRISPR knockout again inducing a stronger reduction in fitness than the
inhibitor.

These data suggested that the frequent co-expression of BCL2L1 and MCL1 could potentially be driving
resistance to single-agent inhibitors of these proteins due to their largely overlapping functional roles in
suppression of apoptosis(41, 42). We tested this possibility by comparing the sensitivity of cancer cell
lines to single agent versus combined small molecule inhibition of both BCL-XL (A1331852 or AZ-3202)
and MCL-1 (AZD-5991) (Figure 1A). The sensitivity of cancer cell lines to this combined inhibition was
striking – this combination potently induced loss of fitness across virtually all cancer types and was
evident in multiple independently-generated datasets (Figure 1A). The combination treatment produced
synergistic cell death in the majority of cancer types and was highly effective even in cancer cell lines
harboring TP53 loss-of-function (LOF) mutations (Figure S1B). There was even a trend toward increased
sensitivity in TP53 LOF mutants in certain cancers including non-small cell lung cancer, glioma, and
invasive breast cancer (Figure S1B).

We next sought to directly measure the induction of apoptotic cell death in response to BCL-2, BCL-XL
and MCL-1 inhibitors to test for synergistic cell death. We treated a panel of 132 cancer cell lines of
diverse origins with the BCL-2 inhibitor ABT-199, the BCL-XL inhibitor A1331852, the MCL-1 inhibitor
S63845 or combinations thereof. While we detected only limited apoptotic cell death (as measured by
Annexin V staining and flow cytometry analysis) in response to ABT-199 (largely in hematologic
cancers), the BCL-XL inhibitor induced apoptosis in multiple solid cancer types including ovarian, lung,
renal, breast, colon and liver cancer cell lines but only reached 50% cell death in 12.8% of cell lines
(Figure 1K). MCL-1 inhibition was similarly active in subsets of multiple solid cancer cell lines, but the
sensitivity was again highly heterogeneous and only induced over 50% cell death in 13.6% of cell lines.

We next evaluated the responses to combination treatments targeting BCL-2 family proteins. We found
that combining ABT-199 with A1331852 induced a similar level of apoptosis as the ABT-199 or
A1331852 alone, suggesting that the additional targeting of BCL-2 in BCL-XL dependent cancers, or vice
versa, does not enhance apoptosis significantly (Figure 1K). This was also evident from Bliss synergy
scores, which showed that A1331852 and ABT-199 induced synergistic cell death (Excess Over Bliss
[EOB] score >20) in only 15.9% of cell lines, mostly in hematologic malignancies (Figure S1C).
Combining ABT-199 and S63845 produced synergistic cell death in 65.2% of cell lines with an average
EOB of 39.41, indicating that combining MCL-1 and BCL-2 inhibition would potently induce cell death
across many cancer types. However, this extent of synergy was only evident at the highest dose of BH3
mimetics (1 μM), with lower doses of 0.1 μM only producing synergy in 30.3% of cell lines with an
average EOB of 17.3. In contrast, combination treatment with A1331852 and S63845 induced synergistic
apoptosis in 88.6% of cell lines (average EOB = 59.4), including highly chemoresistant cancer types such
as glioblastoma multiforme (GBM), ovarian, pancreas, liver, colon and melanoma (Figure 1K and S1C).
The synergy was also evident at lower doses including 0.1 μM of each agent (57.6% synergy, average
EOB of 40.2). Note that knockout of BAX and BAK1 completely eliminated HeLa cell sensitivity to this
combination treatment, which is consistent with an on-target effect of these agents (Figure 1K).

Taken together, these data demonstrate that concurrent inhibition of BCL-XL and MCL-1 synergistically
induces apoptosis across a broad range of cancer types, including those that are highly chemoresistant.
This is in agreement with some previous studies showing that this combination can effectively treat
otherwise intractable malignancies including GBM(43). However, we and others have recently shown that
concurrent inhibition of BCL-XL and MCL-1 is not tolerated in vivo due to severe toxicity in the liver,
lungs and vasculature(44, 45).

Epithelial ovarian cancer cell lines are dependent on BCL-XL for survival

We next sought to better understand responses to single-agent BH3 mimetics and the mechanisms that
drive resistance. We chose to focus our investigation on HGSOC due to several factors including the high
mortality rate of this disease and our in vitro analysis showing consistent, yet modest sensitivity to single-
agent BH3 mimetics, especially A1331852 targeting BCL-XL (Figure 1K). We first measured apoptotic
priming (proximity to the apoptotic threshold) and dependence on pro-survival proteins by performing
BH3 profiling on a panel of ten well-validated HGSOC cell lines, each with mutant p53(46). All of the
cell lines initiated mitochondrial apoptosis (as indicated by release of cytochrome c) in response to high
doses (100 μM) of BIM and BID BH3 peptides (Figure 2A and S2A). These peptides are able to inhibit
all pro-survival BCL-2 family proteins and also activate BAX and BAK directly(24, 47). Differences
between these cell lines were evident at lower doses – the most primed of the cell lines were OVCAR8,
OVCAR3 and OVKATE cells. Moderately primed cell lines included JHOM-1, KURAMOCHI, OAW28,
OV90, OVCAR4 and TYK-nu cells. OVSAHO cells were least primed, as evidenced by their limited
release of cytochrome c in response to the highest doses of pro-apoptotic BIM and BID BH3 peptides.
These differences were consistent with the responses to the PUMA BH3 peptide, which can also inhibit
all pro-survival proteins but cannot directly activate BAX and BAK(48) – OVSAHO cells were again the
most resistant to this pro-apoptotic BH3 peptide. These results indicated that most HGSOC cell lines
maintain sensitivity to pro-apoptotic signals and would be expected to trigger this form of cell death in
response to stress or damage that produces pro-apoptotic signaling.

Given that HGSOC cell lines were primed for apoptosis, we next sought to determine if these cell lines
exhibit dependence on pro-survival proteins from the BCL-2 family. Using BH3 peptides that selectively
inhibit each of these BCL-2 family pro-survival proteins(48), we detected consistent dependence on the
pro-survival protein BCL-XL in all OvCa cell lines except JHOM-1, OVSAHO and TYK-nu (Figure 2B
and S2A). This was evident based on the sensitivity (cytochrome c release) of most OvCa cell lines to the
BAD BH3 peptide that selectively inhibits BCL-2, BCL-XL and BCL-W, as well as high sensitivity to the
HRK BH3 peptide, which selectively inhibits BCL-XL(48). There was relatively low sensitivity to the
MCL-1 inhibiting peptide MS1, demonstrating that this dependence is relatively weak and less
consistently evident.

To further evaluate dependencies on pro-survival proteins, we tested OvCa cell line sensitivity to a
broader panel of BH3 mimetics that selectively inhibit certain pro-survival proteins. Consistent with the
BH3 profiling results and earlier chemosensitivity analyses, we detected that many HGSOC cell lines
exhibited a loss of viability in response to BH3 mimetics that inhibit BCL-XL including ABT-263 and
A1331852 as well as DT2216, a BCL-XL selective PROTAC/degrader, but not the selective BCL-2
inhibitor ABT-199 or the selective MCL-1 inhibitor S63845 (except OVCAR4 cells) (Figure 2C).
Furthermore, flow cytometric analysis of cells stained with the phosphatidylserine (PS) binding agent
Annexin V (AxV) and DNA binding agent propidium iodide (PI) indicated that cells were undergoing
apoptosis (Figure S2B). This is demonstrated by cells becoming positive for AxV prior to becoming
positive for PI since the latter agent only enters cells with a disrupted plasma membrane, which occurs
after externalization of PS in cells undergoing caspase-mediated apoptosis(49). When comparing the BH3
profiling with mimetic sensitivity analysis, Spearman’s rho correlation testing indicated that
mitochondrial sensitivity to the BAD BH3 peptide correlated with cellular sensitivity to agents targeting
BCL-XL including ABT-263 and DT2216 (Figure 2D). This confirms that BH3 profiling can detect
dependence on pro-survival proteins in OvCa cells, as has been shown previously in other settings(23,
50–53), and indicates that BCL-XL is a potentially targetable dependency in HGSOC.

We next tested the extent to which these dependencies would be evident in an even broader set of OvCa
cell lines. We compared the reduction of fitness across 58 OvCa cell lines in which each of the five major
pro-survival genes are knocked out via CRISPR, again from the DepMap initiative(40). None of the 58
cell lines were dependent on BCL2, BCL2A1 (encoding A1) or BCL2L2 (encoding BCL-W) for
maintaining fitness (Figure 2E). Four of the cell lines (6.9%) exhibited a loss of fitness in response to
knockout of MCL1, indicating that this gene is considered essential in a small subset of OvCa cell lines.
In contrast to the lack of widespread dependence on other pro-survival proteins, knockout of BCL2L1
(encoding BCL-XL) resulted in a loss of fitness in 28 of 58 (48.3%) lines, indicating that this gene is
essential for survival for about half of all OvCa cell lines (Figure 2E). The high dependence on this pro-
survival protein in the CRISPR screen was consistent with the high expression of BCL2L1 transcripts
across OvCa cell lines (Figure 2F). Furthermore, a comparison of the effects on fitness in OvCa cell lines
undergoing RNAi-mediated knockdown of pro-survival genes confirmed that only BCL2L1 is an essential
gene in the OvCa cell lines tested (Figure 2G). Consistent with the higher expression of BCL2L1 in OvCa
being an indicator of potential dependence, we found that mRNA expression of this gene is correlated
with its essentiality (Figure 2H). Interestingly, high expression of MCL1 transcripts was associated with
reduced essentiality of BCL2L1 (Figure 2I), suggesting that MCL-1 can potentially compensate for the
loss of BCL-XL, which is consistent with our pan-cancer data showing that dual inhibition of these
proteins is necessary for robust cell death.

Epithelial ovarian cancers undergo rapid apoptotic adaptation in response to BCL-XL inhibition

Our results thus far indicate that OvCa cell lines express BCL2L1 at relatively high levels and are broadly
dependent on BCL-XL when it is targeted via CRISPR (Figure 2E). However, despite their potency and
selectivity, single-agent inhibitors of BCL-XL induced only modest levels of apoptosis (Figure 2C). To
gain insight into what may be preventing stronger apoptosis induction, we evaluated expression of BCL-2
family proteins in response to these agents. We focused on ABT-263 and DT2216 since these are both
being evaluated in the clinic. These agents have similar structures with only one major alteration: DT2216
adds a VHL ligand to the ABT-263 structure to drive VHL-mediated BCL-XL degradation instead of
competitive inhibition(37). This alteration was introduced to avoid the on-target thrombocytopenia
induced by ABT-263 in patients since platelets express very limited levels of VHL and do not degrade
BCL-XL when treated with DT2216(37). We first confirmed that the BCL-XL degrading agent DT2216
rapidly and selectively reduces expression of BCL-XL (Figure 2J) and triggers caspase-mediated,
apoptotic cell death, which can be prevented by the pan-caspase inhibitor QVD-OPH (Figure S2C) or by
knocking out the apoptosis effectors BAX and BAK (Figure S2D). Surprisingly, we also detected a mild
yet consistent increase in expression levels of pro-survival MCL-1 protein in DT2216-treated cells,
especially at the 4- and 8-hour time points (Figure 2J), as well as BCL-2 and BCL-W, albeit less
consistently. This indicates that changes in expression levels of BCL-2 family proteins favoring cell
survival occur in response to BCL-XL targeting – a phenomenon we refer to as “rapid apoptotic
adaptation” (RAA). We directly compared this adaptation between ABT-263 and DT2216 and found that
while mild upregulation of MCL-1 was evident in response to both agents, treatment with ABT-263 also
caused upregulation of BCL-XL, which would also block induction of apoptosis (Figure 2K). This
upregulation of BCL-XL was completely absent in DT2216 treated cells, presumably due to the ongoing
degradation facilitated by this agent. This suggests that the degree of RAA is inherently limited by agents
that degrade pro-survival proteins instead of inhibiting them. Furthermore, BH3 profiling of OvCa cells
treated with ABT-263 or DT2216 demonstrated that although both agents increased apoptotic priming,
ABT-263 treated cells exhibited a reduction in apoptotic priming and dependence on MCL-1 after 48
hours whereas DT2216 cells did not (Figure S2E-F). The enhanced apoptosis-promoting activity of
DT2216 compared with ABT-263 was also evident in response to low-dose treatment with these agents
for even longer periods (Figure S2G-H).

Quantitative PCR (qPCR) analysis on DT2216- and ABT-263-treated cells showed a lack of changes in
transcription of genes including BCL2, BCL2L1, MCL1 and BCL2L2, suggesting that the RAA is
potentially driven by altered translation or degradation of these pro-survival proteins (Figure S2I). To
gain insight into the molecular signaling that facilitates these changes, we performed reverse phase
protein array (RPPA) analysis on cells treated with a low dose of DT2216 to evaluate the expression of
over 500 proteins and their phosphorylation states(54). The RPPA analysis demonstrated that heat shock
protein 27 (HSP27) was being phosphorylated in response to DT2216 treatment (Figure S2J). HSP27 is a
molecular chaperone that has been previously found to stall apoptosis execution by binding and
inactivating caspase 3 or cytochrome c(55, 56). We tested this directly in our DT2216 treated cells and
detected an upregulation of HSP27 and also its phosphorylation at Ser82, an indicator of its active
response to stress(57) upon DT2216 treatment (Figure 2L). HSP27 activity can be inhibited by the small
molecule J2, which prevents its normal activation and binding interactions(58). In agreement with the
involvement of HSP27 in the RAA induced by BH3 mimetics, pretreatment of HGSOC cells with J2
eliminated the upregulation of pro-survival proteins in response to ABT-263 and DT2216 (Figure 2M).
Furthermore, J2 enhanced the apoptosis induced by these BH3 mimetics in OVCAR3, OVCAR4 and
OVCAR8 cells but not OV90 (Figure 2N). Notably, subsequent analysis of HSP27 expression in these
cell lines showed that this protein is widely expressed in HGSOC cell lines but not present in OV90 cells
(Figure 2O), which explains the lack of sensitization to BH3 mimetics by J2 in this cell line. These data
indicate that targeting this protein may forestall RAA and enhance the apoptosis induced by inhibitors of
pro-survival BCL-2 family proteins.

Paclitaxel treatment indirectly targets MCL-1, synergizes with BCL-XL inhibition

Armed with our new understanding of mechanisms of resistance to BH3 mimetic treatment, we next
sought to uncover potential treatment strategies to exploit the synergy of dual targeting of BCL-XL and
MCL-1 while avoiding the toxicity. Due to the short half-life of the MCL-1 protein, we hypothesized that
certain cytotoxic or targeted chemotherapeutic agents may be able to reduce expression of MCL-1 and, in
so doing, indirectly inhibit this protein, which may potentially be better tolerated in vivo when combined
with direct BCL-XL inhibition. We again chose to perform these experiments in HGSOC cell lines due to
their limited sensitivity to single agent inhibitors and synergistic sensitivity to combined inhibition, which
reflected the majority of solid cancer cell lines in our analysis.

We therefore treated the OVCAR3 cell line, which is a well-characterized model of chemoresistant and
p53-mutant HGSOC(46), with a range of cytotoxic and targeted chemotherapeutic agents at doses that
were 1/50th of their Cmax in patients(44). We measured expression levels of MCL-1 and found that only
one agent led to a decrease in MCL-1 levels: paclitaxel (Figure 3A). Paclitaxel is a commonly used
therapy in OvCa patients that induces cancer cell apoptosis by hyper-stabilizing microtubules and causing
prolonged mitotic arrest(59). Dynamic BH3 profiling of OVCAR3 cells treated with the standard of care
agents, carboplatin and paclitaxel showed that both of these agents increase apoptotic priming, indicating
that treated cells are experiencing apoptotic stress (Figure 3B). However, treatment with sensitizer BH3
peptides demonstrated that dependence on BCL-XL is strongly enhanced by treatment with paclitaxel but
not carboplatin (Figure 3C). Interestingly, we also saw evidence of increased MCL-1 dependence in
OVCAR3 cells upon paclitaxel treatment, which is also consistent with the reduced but not completely
eliminated expression of this protein in paclitaxel-treated cells (Figure 3A).

Based on these results, we reasoned that combination treatment with paclitaxel and BCL-XL inhibitors
would be particularly effective at inducing apoptosis in cancer cells. As we had anticipated, we detected
increased rates of apoptosis in OvCa cell lines treated with paclitaxel in combination with multiple BCL-
XL inhibitors including the BCL-2/BCL-W/BCL-XL inhibitor ABT-263 (navitoclax), the BCL-XL
inhibitor A1331852, the BCL-XL PROTAC degrader DT2216 but not BH3 mimetics targeting solely
BCL-2 (ABT-199 [venetoclax]) or MCL-1 (S63845) (Figure 3D). The cell death induced by this
combination was found to be strongly synergistic across a wide range of doses of DT2216 and paclitaxel
across multiple HGSOC cell lines (Figure S3A-B). Two- and three-drug combination chemosensitivity
and synergy analyses showed that while DT2216 was more synergistic with paclitaxel than carboplatin,
there was evidence of synergy across either of those combinations (Figure S3B), suggesting that DT2216
would also synergistically enhance carboplatin-induced cell death. Interestingly, we also detected
antagonism between paclitaxel and carboplatin (Figure S3B), which has been reported previously(60).
However, we found that DT2216 treatment was able to overcome this antagonism (as evidenced by the
responses from treatment with DT2216 with paclitaxel at 10nM and carboplatin at 100μM, paclitaxel at
30nM and carboplatin at 25, 50 or 100 μM) (Figure S3B). Furthermore, the strongest overall induction of
apoptosis was evident when all three agents were combined. Together, these data suggest that DT2216
may be effectively combined with both paclitaxel and carboplatin and may reverse the antagonism that
these agents have when used in combination.
We next confirmed that the cell death induced by DT2216 plus paclitaxel was apoptosis by showing its
inhibition with the pan-caspase inhibitor QVD-OPH (Figure S3C-F). In addition, colony formation assays
showed that while single agent treatment with paclitaxel or DT2216 slightly reduced OVCAR3 colony
growth, combined treatment with both agents completely abrogated the outgrowth of colonies across
multiple HGSOC cell lines (Figure 3E, S3G). This was also evident with the potent BCL-XL inhibitor
A1331852 in combination with paclitaxel and, to a lesser extent, ABT-263 but not in combinations with
selective MCL-1 (S63845) or BCL-2 (ABT-199) inhibitors (Figure 3E). We also found that combined
BCL-XL and MCL-1 inhibition prevented long-term colony outgrowth (Figure S3G), confirming that
simultaneous targeting of these two proteins is sufficient to eliminate HGSOC cells while targeting them
alone is not.

Since we had detected that concurrent targeting of MCL-1 and BCL-XL induced apoptosis across major
solid cancer cell lines, we sought to test the extent to which the indirect targeting of MCL-1 via paclitaxel
treatment could synergize with BCL-XL inhibition in other cancer types. Treatment of 55 cancer cell lines
across 12 different cancer types showed that paclitaxel synergized with the BCL-XL degrader DT2216 in
the vast majority of cases, as detected with Annexin V staining (Figure 3F). In fact, all 55 cell lines,
including those with WT as well as mutant TP53, exhibited synergy in at least one dose combination with
an average excess over Bliss of 10.7 and a range of -19.1 to 68.0 (Figure S3H).

To further investigate how broadly active this combination therapy may be, we evaluated the degree of
synergy that is induced by combination treatment with paclitaxel and ABT-263 in breast, colon and
pancreas cancer cell lines within the Genomics of Drug Sensitivity in Cancer (GDSC) database.
Synergistic cell death was induced by this combination treatment in 49% of the 52 breast cancer cell lines
tested (Figure 3G, S3I) and this was one of the most synergistic of the 1,275 drug combinations tested.
Remarkably, the only agents that exhibited stronger synergy with ABT-263 than paclitaxel were the
aurora A kinase inhibitor alisertib(61) (63% of cell lines exhibiting synergy), ROCK inhibitor
GSK269962A(62) (50%), and pan-Aurora inhibitors Tozasertib(63) (58%), and ZM447439(64) (55%) –
each of these agents has been shown to induce cell cycle arrest in G2/M phase and thus have a similar
mechanism of apoptosis induction as paclitaxel. This indicates that regardless of how it is induced,
prolonged mitotic arrest is expected to consistently lead to depletion of MCL-1 and sensitization to BCL-
XL inhibition.

Extending this analysis to colon cancer, we again found that ABT-263 synergizes with several of the 26
agents used in the GDSC Anchor(65) combination screen but the strongest synergy was detected with
vinorelbine (36% of 45 cell lines) (Figure 3H, S3I) – similarly to paclitaxel, which was not one of the
compounds in this screen, this vinca alkaloid induces apoptosis primarily by disrupting microtubules and
triggering mitotic arrest(66). Finally, combination screening in pancreas cancer cell lines showed that
ABT-263 synergized most often with paclitaxel (71% of 29 cell lines) (Figure 3I, S3I).

We then compared the extent of synergy that is evident specifically with navitoclax across the full range
of combination treatments in the three cancer types. We found that navitoclax most consistently induces
synergistic cell death when used in combination with agents that target mitosis (Figure 3J), which
includes AURKA, AURKB and AURKC inhibitors as well as microtubule stabilizers and destabilizers
(Figure S3I). These data indicate that treatment with paclitaxel or other agents that induce mitotic arrest
consistently synergizes with BCL-XL inhibition – this may therefore be a broadly active therapeutic
combination.

To gain insight into the mechanisms that are responsible for this synergistic apoptosis, we evaluated
expression of BCL-2 family proteins in OVCAR3 cells treated with paclitaxel ± DT2216. Consistent with
our previous results, we found that protein expression levels of BCL-XL were reduced in a dose-
dependent manner by DT2216 (Figure 3K). We also detected a consistent increase in MCL-1 expression
in OVCAR3 cells treated with DT2216 at three different concentrations (Figure 3K), reflecting RAA.
This was also evident in the other cell lines treated with DT2216 (Figure 3L), reflecting RAA. In contrast,
paclitaxel treatment consistently decreased MCL-1 protein expression, which was not driven by
transcriptional changes (Figure S4A) and would be expected to cause the increased priming and BCL-XL
dependence that we detected (Figure 3A, 3K-L). Interestingly, we also detected an increase in BCL-XL
expression and a second, higher-weight protein band in the immunoblot for BCL-XL in cells treated with
paclitaxel (Figure 3K-L), which we reasoned may be indicative of a phosphorylation event. BCL-XL
phosphorylation at Ser62 has been previously found to inactivate the pro-survival activity of this protein
and is likely also contributing to the increased priming and BCL-XL dependence we detected in
paclitaxel-treated cells(67). Using a phospho-specific antibody (antibody specificity confirmed using
siRNA-mediated knockdown in Figure S4B), we detected phosphorylation of BCL-XL at Ser62 in
paclitaxel-treated OVCAR3 cells (Figure 3A, 3L) but not in cells treated with other agents (Figure 3A),
confirming that this inactivating event is occurring in response to microtubule targeting with this agent.
Notably, we also detected decreased HSP27 expression in cells treated with paclitaxel (Figure 2L),
suggesting that paclitaxel treatment also enhances BCL-XL inhibitor-induced apoptosis by preventing
HSP27-enabled RAA – this effect on HSP27 may enhance sensitivity to other combination treatment
agents as well.

We next sought to test whether the enhancement of apoptosis in DT2216-treated cells is due to on-target
BCL-XL inhibition by knocking down BCL2L1 (encoding BCL-XL) in OVCAR3 cells (Figure S4B) and
testing cell sensitivity to BH3 mimetics as well as paclitaxel. siRNA knockdown of BCL2L1 induced
apoptosis compared with non-targeting siRNA and eliminated cellular sensitivity to subsequent treatment
with BCL-XL inhibitors including DT2216 (Figure S4C). Interestingly, we also detected increased
sensitivity to the MCL-1 inhibitor S63845 in cells transfected with BCL2L1 siRNA (Figure S4C), which
aligns with our experiments showing that co-targeting MCL-1 and BCL-XL leads to increased apoptosis
compared with either agent alone. Finally, BCL2L1 knockdown sensitized cells to paclitaxel treatment
similarly to BCL-XL inhibition with no increased apoptosis from additional treatment with DT2216
(Figure S4C).

Finally, we tested the extent to which DT2216 would synergize with other agents that can trigger mitotic
arrest beyond paclitaxel. To test this, we measured the sensitivity of a panel of GBM, NSCLC, breast and
ovarian cancer cell lines to combination treatment with DT2216 and the AURKA inhibitor Alisertib(67)
(Figure 4A and S5A), the PLK1 inhibitor BI-2536(68) (Figure 4B and S5B), and the microtubule-
targeting agents vincristine(69) (Figure 4C and S5C) and docetaxel(70) (Figure 4D and S5D) using
Annexin V staining and flow cytometry after 72 hour treatment. Combination treatments were more
effective than single agents and exhibited synergy across each of these common cancer types as
determined by excess over Bliss analysis (Figure S5A-D). These data confirmed that the apoptosis
induced by agents that cause mitotic arrest can be enhanced by BCL-XL inhibition and DT2216 treatment
in particular and suggest that combining DT2216 with these and other mitotic arrest-promoting agents in
other cancer types would be broadly effective.

Antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs) are targeted cytotoxic agents that bind tumor-associated surface
antigens, undergo endocytic internalization, and release intracellular payloads that induce apoptotic cell
death(68). More than half of FDA-approved ADCs use microtubule-disrupting payloads, including DM4,
MMAE, and MMAF, and suppression of apoptosis is a prominent mechanism of resistance to this class of
agents(68). We therefore tested whether BH3 mimetics or DT2216 could potentiate apoptosis induced by
ADC payloads. Across a concentration range of MMAF, BCL-XL inhibition or degradation, but not
MCL-1 or BCL-2 inhibition, enhanced apoptosis (Figure 4E) in a synergistic manner (Figure S5E).
DT2216 similarly synergized with other microtubule-targeting ADC payloads tested (Fig. 4F and Fig.
S5F), suggesting that BCL-XL degradation may enhance ADC payload-induced apoptosis and overcome
apoptotic resistance to ADC therapy.
BCL-XL restrains BAX, BAK and BIM in HGSOC cells

The consistent synergy between mitotic arrest-inducing agents and DT2216 prompted us to investigate
the binding interactions that lead to induction of apoptosis in response to these combination treatments.
By immunoprecipitating BCL-XL and blotting for BCL-2 family proteins in OVCAR3 cells without any
treatment, we detected active binding of pro-apoptotic proteins including BIM, BAK and BAX, indicating
active sequestration of multiple proteins that are critical for initiation of apoptosis (Figure 5A). In cells
treated with paclitaxel, BCL-XL continued to bind these pro-apoptotic proteins while levels of MCL-1
were reduced, contributing to increased BCL-XL dependence. Low-dose DT2216 treatment led to BCL-
XL degradation, which freed the majority of bound BIM, BAK and BAX. Combination treatment further
exacerbated these changes, with only minimal BIM remaining bound to BCL-XL and BAX and BAK
being released to carry out apoptosis initiation. Immunoprecipitation of MCL-1 under these same
conditions clarified the mechanism of apoptosis induction. In untreated cells, MCL-1 actively bound BIM
and BAK but not BAX (Figure 5B). Treatment with paclitaxel reduced the expression of MCL-1 while
the remaining protein continued to bind BIM and BAK, again contributing to increased priming and
dependence on both BCL-XL and MCL-1. Treatment with DT2216 alone forced MCL-1 to bind and
sequester additional BIM that was freed from the degraded BCL-XL. Combination treatment causes a
reduction in MCL-1 levels along with reduced ability for BCL-XL to sequester the freed pro-apoptotic
signals, thus triggering apoptosis.

Prolonged mitotic arrest is associated with increased BCL-XL dependence

Since paclitaxel is known to disrupt microtubule dynamics and cause mitotic arrest in cancer cells, we
next sought to compare the levels of apoptotic priming and dependencies in cycling versus arrested cells.
We first confirmed that paclitaxel treatment causes mitotic arrest of OvCa cells (Figure S6A) and then
subjected the paclitaxel-treated cells to BH3 profiling. Notably, we detected a major difference in priming
between cell populations: in the presence of paclitaxel, non-mitotic cells exhibited lower levels of priming
than untreated cells while those cells that were undergoing mitosis were much more primed (Figure 5C).
We also detected reduced BCL-XL dependence in the non-mitotic cells while those cells that were
arrested in mitosis were much more dependent on BCL-XL and, to a lesser extent, MCL-1 (Figure 5D).
Immunoblotting analysis uncovered the likely molecular mechanism driving these changes: expression of
MCL-1 is nearly absent in cells that are arrested in mitosis as a result of paclitaxel treatment while BCL-
XL levels are largely maintained (Figure 5E). Taken together, these data indicate that prolonged mitotic
arrest is responsible for driving reduced MCL-1 expression and convergence of pro-survival dependencies
onto BCL-XL, setting the stage for potent induction of apoptosis in response to BCL-XL inhibition.

Ovarian patient tumors express BCL-XL, especially HGSOC

Since we found that high expression of BCL2L1 transcripts is correlated with BCL-XL dependence, we
asked whether BCL-XL protein was detectable in patient tumors. Using publicly available databases, we
found that BCL-XL is expressed specifically in tumor cells within a majority of OvCa patient tumors
available for analysis, including 6/6 HGSOC (Figure S6B-C). Half of HGSOC tumors expressed BCL-XL
in over 75% of tumor cells with the remaining tumors expressing this protein in 25-75% of tumor cells
(Figure S6B, S6D). In all cases where BCL-XL was expressed in tumor cells, including in mucinous,
endometroid and serous OvCa, expression of the protein was found to be cytoplasmic or membranous
(Figure S6B, S6E), consistent with the pro-survival effect that this protein has while interacting with other
BCL-2 family proteins on the outer mitochondrial membrane.

We also sought to compare expression levels of BCL-2 family genes in primary HGSOC and how they
are altered by neoadjuvant therapy to understand how tumors evolve under therapeutic pressure. Analysis
of an existing gene expression dataset from 20 patients with advanced stage (IIIC or IV) HGSOC prior to
and after receiving neoadjuvant chemotherapy(69) showed that pro-survival gene expression largely
mirrored the OvCa cell line data (Figure 2F) with BCL2L1, BCL2L2 and MCL1 being consistently
expressed at high levels (Figure S6F-G). Pro-apoptotic genes including BAD and BCL2L11 (BIM) were
consistently detected, as were the critical pore-forming proteins BAX and BAK1 (Figure S6G).
Interestingly, neoadjuvant therapy, which consisted of carboplatin and paclitaxel, did not significantly
alter the expression of BCL-2 family genes in tumor cells except for MCL1, which was upregulated post
neoadjuvant chemotherapy (Figure S6F-G). We also did not detect any significant associations between
expression of specific BCL-2 family genes with outcomes in response to neoadjuvant chemotherapy
(Figure S6H). Taken together, these data indicate that BCL-XL is a consistently expressed and targetable
apoptotic dependency in HGSOC. The increase in MCL1 detected in post-therapy specimens may result
in increased resistance to BCL-XL inhibition, suggesting that treatment-naïve HGSOC tumors will be
most susceptible to BCL-XL inhibition.

Ovarian primary tumors are dependent on BCL-XL for survival

Cancer cell lines can accumulate alterations during in vitro culture that limit their effectiveness as disease
models(46, 70, 71). To address this, we performed BH3 profiling, which does not require any ex vivo
culture, and chemosensitivity analysis on a panel of freshly-collected tumors including eleven HGSOC
tumor specimens and one low-grade serous ovarian carcinoma (LGSOC) specimen from patients at
different phases of treatment (Table S1). OvCa cells from each of the twelve specimens were responsive
to pro-apoptotic BH3 peptides even at low or moderate concentrations (BIM BH3 peptide at 1 μM and 10
μM, for example), indicating that cells from these tumors can readily initiate apoptosis in response to low
or moderate levels of pro-apoptotic signaling (Figure 5F). Even tumor specimens from patients with
recurrent disease continued to be primed for apoptosis – this suggests that targeting the apoptosis pathway
could be effective even in patients relapsing after initial therapy. Notably, the LGSOC specimen was
among the least sensitive to the pro-apoptotic peptides, indicating that this tumor exhibits lower
dependence on pro-survival proteins (Figure 5F). LGSOC tumors typically have lower proliferation rates
than HGSOC, lack TP53 mutations and exhibit more frequent mutations in KRAS, BRAF, NRAS or
ERBB2 genes to stimulate MAPK signaling(72, 73).

Next, we evaluated responses to the sensitizer peptides that can indicate dependence on individual BCL-2
family pro-survival proteins. We detected 10% or greater cytochrome c release in response to the BAD
BH3 peptide (100 μM) that inhibits BCL-2, BCL-W and BCL-XL in 11 of 12 OvCa tumor specimens
compared to DMSO treatment (average difference: 22.3%; range: 4.01 – 68.2%) (Figure 5G). We
detected BCL-XL dependence using the HRK BH3 peptide (>10% cytochrome c release compared to
DMSO) also in 11 of 12 tumors, including treatment naïve, neoadjuvant and recurrent patients (average
difference: 28.0%; range: 9.2 – 66.0%). The only tumor that did not show >10% cytochrome c release in
response to the HRK BH3 peptide was the LGSOC specimen – although this suggests that low-grade
serous tumors may exhibit less BCL-XL dependence, additional specimens would need to be analyzed to
determine if this was a generalizable finding. Finally, we also detected dependence on MCL-1 in ten of
twelve specimens (Figure 5G) based on responses to the MS1 peptide (30 μM) (average difference:
29.8%, range: 7.4 – 54.2%).

Consistent with the HGSOC cell line data, using dynamic BH3 profiling on HGSOC specimen DF4379
that contained sufficient cell numbers for this analysis, we found that paclitaxel treatment increased
primary OvCa cell dependence on BCL-XL, as indicated by increased cytochrome c release in response to
the BAD and HRK BH3 peptides (Figure 5H-I).

Ex vivo chemosensitivity analysis of the freshly-collected primary tumors was consistent with BH3
profiling data and indicated that BCL-XL inhibition or degradation with single-agent treatment with
A1331852, ABT-263 or DT2216 induced modest levels of apoptosis (Figure 5J), as did inhibition of
MCL-1 with S63845 in certain tumors. We also confirmed that combination treatment with paclitaxel and
BCL-XL inhibitors more potently induced apoptosis in DF4379 (Figure 5K), consistent with the dynamic
BH3 profiling results (Figure 5H-I).

We next evaluated BCL-2 family protein changes in HGSOC primary tumors in response to paclitaxel
and DT2216 treatment ex vivo. We confirmed that DT2216 treatment led to the rapid and preferential
degradation of BCL-XL in primary HSGOC tumor cells (Figure 5L), which was further enhanced in the
presence of paclitaxel. We also noted an increase in MCL-1 expression in response to DT2216 treatment,
indicating that RAA also occurs in primary tumor cells. Similar effects, including the rapid degradation of
BCL-XL, by DT2216 and paclitaxel-mediated phosphorylation of this protein were observed in the
DF5053 specimen (Figure 5M). Interestingly, paclitaxel treatment did not induce detectable
downregulation of MCL-1 in primary specimens, which is likely due to the very limited ex vivo
proliferation that primary tumor cells undergo ex vivo that limits paclitaxel effectiveness, as previously
reported(74).

Ovarian patient-derived organoids are dependent on BCL-XL for survival after therapy-induced
apoptotic stress

Patient-derived organoids (PDOs) provide a platform for assessment of tumor cell sensitivity to therapy in
tumor cells grown as spheroids, which more accurately reflects the morphology of patient tumors and can
be more predictive of in vivo therapy efficacy(75). We tested the sensitivity of five HGSOC PDO models
that reflect different treatment histories, from treatment-naïve to recurrent (Table S2), to combination
therapy including paclitaxel and DT2216 and assessed death by quantifying apoptosis via AxV/7AAD
staining. The treatment-naïve PDO, 18-47, was mildly sensitive to paclitaxel treatment and exhibited
more sensitivity to low-dose treatment with DT2216 (Figure 6A-B). Impressively, the combination
treatment was most effective and induced more apoptosis than would be expected based on additivity
alone (Figure 6A-B). The neoadjuvant treated PDO, 17-116, exhibited very limited sensitivity to either of
the agents alone but again the combination treatment resulted in enhanced apoptosis. The two heavily
pretreated, recurrent samples exhibited some divergence in sensitivity: 17-39 exhibited no sensitivity to
DT2216 alone but, when DT2216 was combined with paclitaxel, there was enhancement of apoptosis.
Finally, the 17-121 specimen exhibited very limited sensitivity to paclitaxel while being sensitive to
single-agent DT2216 and even more sensitive to combination treatment. These tests of apoptotic cell
death were consistent with results obtained via ATP measurement (Figure 6C). Overall, these data
demonstrated that HGSOC cells, when incubated in tumor-like organoids, continued to exhibit
dependence on BCL-XL, especially in combination with paclitaxel treatment.

Ovarian PDX models are dependent on BCL-XL, sensitive to DT2216

Short-term ex vivo culture of OvCa PDX models allows for rapid evaluation of therapeutic efficacy
across a diverse range of HGSOC models that exhibit immunohistological and molecular fidelity to the
original patient tumor(76). We collected ascites from eight luciferized ovarian cancer PDX models (Table
3) generated from patients with varying clinical and genetic characteristics including prior treatment
history (ranging from treatment-naïve to 7 prior lines of therapy), BRCA status (WT as well as BRCA1
mutant), and HGSOC-associated mutations (e.g., TP53, PTEN, CDKN2A) that were growing in immuno-
compromised mice. These OvCa cells were cultured in vitro for two days and then treated with DT2216
and paclitaxel. Four days later, cell viability was evaluated by adding luciferin and measuring
luminescence. We noted that even heavily pretreated PDX models (e.g., DF14 and DF68 – 5 prior lines of
therapy; DF181 – 7 prior lines) were highly sensitive to DT2216 treatment alone (Figure 6D). This
suggests that HGSOC cells can be dependent on BCL-XL regardless of prior treatment status, which is in
agreement with our HGSOC patient tumor data (Figure 5G). The results in DF68 and DF181 were
particularly noteworthy given their resistance to paclitaxel treatment, presumably due to their extensive
prior treatment with this agent. Five of the PDX models (63%) exhibited sensitivity to single agent
DT2216, as defined by 33% or greater loss of viability in response to DT2216 treatment at 0.71 μM. We
also noted that half (4/8) of the PDX models were consistently more sensitive to DT2216 than paclitaxel
at equivalent doses. Finally, we detected synergy between DT2216 and paclitaxel treatment in 7/8 PDX
models (Figure 6D, S7A-B).

Ovarian xenograft tumors are eradicated with combination treatment targeting BCL-XL and
microtubules

We next set out to test the efficacy and safety of combination therapy targeting microtubules with
paclitaxel and BCL-XL inhibition/degradation in vivo. The on-target and dose-limiting thrombocytopenia
induced by small molecule inhibitors of BCL-XL has prohibited the clinical utilization of these
agents(77). To reduce the likelihood of this issue precluding the clinical evaluation of the synergistic anti-
OvCa activity of BCL-XL inhibition in combination with paclitaxel, we compared the sensitivity of mouse
and human platelets to BH3 mimetics targeting BCL-2 family proteins in vitro. We found that mouse
platelets were largely insensitive to inhibitors of BCL-2 or MCL-1, as indicated by stable platelet counts
after 48 hours of treatment, but were highly sensitive to inhibitors of BCL-XL except the BCL-XL
PROTAC DT2216, which did not significantly affect mouse platelet viability (Figure 7A). Human
platelets exhibited a similar sensitivity pattern to BH3 mimetics but were highly sensitive (>90%
reduction in platelet counts) to even low doses of conventional BCL-XL inhibitors (Figure 7B). However,
DT2216 was significantly less toxic to human platelets than conventional BCL-XL inhibitors ABT-263
and A1331852 and was therefore chosen for in vivo studies.

To evaluate the in vivo efficacy of the combination therapy we identified, we first established xenografts
of the OVCAR3 cell line, which has been a well-validated model of aggressive HGSOC(78), in
immunocompromised NSG (NOD.Cg-Prkdcscid Il2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ) mice. Once tumors reached a size of about
65-100 mm3, we administered paclitaxel at 15 mg/kg every four days via intraperitoneal injection for the
first 12 days, with a total dose of 60 mg/kg per animal, DT2216 at 15 mg/kg every four days, or a
combination of these treatments. These tumors were largely insensitive to paclitaxel treatment, which
only slowed tumor growth (Figure 7C-D) – this is consistent with OVCAR3 resistance to this agent in
vitro (Figure 3D). DT2216 was more effective as a single agent therapy and reduced tumor growth further
but did not induce tumor remissions (Figure 7C-D). Impressively, the combination treatment caused near-
complete tumor regression with only small nodules remaining (Figure 7C-D). Although the animals were
tolerating the DT2216 treatment well, we ceased treatment with DT2216 at day 40 to determine if tumors,
which showed no evidence of growth during continued DT2216 treatment, would relapse. Notably, no
regrowth was detected during the 40-day monitoring period, indicating that the tumor cells had all been
eradicated or incapacitated by the combination treatment (Figure 7C).

To monitor for signs of toxicity, we measured animal weight every other day and performed complete
blood counts every four days. We detected, on average, less than 10% body weight loss in animals treated
with any of the agents including the combination (Figure 7E). Due to the on-target thrombocytopenia that
is caused by inhibition of BCL-XL in patients, we closely monitored platelet counts and found that
DT2216 caused only a detectable reduction in platelet levels after the first dose (Figure 7F). Platelet
levels recovered to a normal range for all subsequent time points despite continuous DT2216 dosing. In
this experiment, while the paclitaxel did not induce any reduction in platelet counts, the combination
treatment of DT2216 with paclitaxel did delay platelet count recovery, which was achieved when DT2216
treatment concluded on day 32.

Further, in animals treated with DT2216, we performed BH3 profiling of tumors at time of euthanasia to
understand how this treatment affects apoptosis sensitivity in tumor cells. Despite treatment having
ceased over 30 days prior to tumor collection, we detected higher levels of apoptotic priming in DT2216
treated tumors compared with vehicle treated controls (Figure 7G). Further, we detected reduced
dependence on BCL-XL in these tumors and, surprisingly, enhanced dependence on MCL-1 (Figure 7H),
suggesting that DT2216 treated tumors had adapted to BCL-XL inhibition by upregulating MCL-1, which
can be attenuated by paclitaxel, as evidenced by the responses to combination treatment.

We then utilized a second HGSOC cell line xenograft model to evaluate responses to a reduced frequency
regimen of paclitaxel with DT2216 to determine if DT2216 could enhance sensitivity to weekly
paclitaxel, which is a common treatment schedule in patients(1). We used the OVCAR8 cells, which are
known to grow aggressively in vivo with HGSOC histology(78). As expected, the tumors did not respond
favorably to paclitaxel treatment and actually exhibited a stronger delay in growth when treated with
DT2216 compared with paclitaxel (Figure 7I), yet there was no tumor regression in response to either of
these treatments. In contrast, the combination therapy was able to induce tumor regression (Figure 7J),
with growth not resuming until the cessation of paclitaxel administration, and improved overall survival
(Figure 7K). This combination was well tolerated and did not induce weight loss (Figure 7L) nor
abnormally low platelet levels (Figure 7M) despite continued treatment with DT2216 until humane
endpoint was reached.

DT2216 eradicates chemoresistant HGSOC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) tumors in
combination with paclitaxel

Finally, we sought to test the activity of paclitaxel and DT2216 in OvCa PDX tumors, which are expected
to more faithfully recapitulate patient tumors than cell line-based xenografts. We first generated in vivo
tumors from five previously-established OvCa PDX models originating from patients that were treatment-
naïve or treated with multiple lines of therapy (Table S3) to assess their levels of priming and BCL-XL
dependence. BH3 profiling of PDX tumor cells indicated that 4/5 of the PDX models were primed for
apoptosis, again indicated by the high degree of cytochrome c release in response to even moderate (10
μM) or low (1 μM) doses of pro-apoptotic BIM and BID BH3 peptides (Figure 8A). Interestingly, the
PDX model that was derived from the patient with the highest number of prior lines of chemotherapy
(DF68 – 5 prior lines of therapy) was also the least primed. We also detected significant release of
cytochrome c in response to the BAD and HRK BH3 peptides in 3/5 PDX models, indicating dependence
on BCL-XL (Figure 8B).

Although the DF20 PDX model was the most highly primed and most dependent on BCL-XL, this model
was not chosen for in vivo studies since it was derived from a patient that was treatment naïve(79). We
intended to test the combination therapy in a recurrent model since recurrent disease is the context in
which most early phase clinical trials are conducted. We therefore performed dynamic BH3 profiling on
the recurrent DF83 model and detected significant increases in apoptotic priming (Figure 8C) and BCL-
XL dependence (Figure 8D) when cells were treated with paclitaxel. We found that the DF83 tumor cells
expressed BCL-XL, albeit at lower levels than other models that had been treated with fewer lines of
chemotherapy (Figure 8E). Based on these characteristics, we chose the DF83 model for in vivo efficacy
studies.

We established growth of DF83 PDX tumors and initiated a similar treatment regimen as used for
OVCAR3 xenografts. Consistent with the prior treatment of these cells with chemotherapy, paclitaxel
treatment only slowed tumor growth in vivo (Figure 8F). DT2216 also had a modest impact on tumor
growth, consistent with potential RAA driving resistance to single-agent treatment. Strikingly, the
combination of paclitaxel and DT2216 induced rapid tumor regressions and eradicated the tumors
completely (Figure 8F-G). We again ceased DT2216 treatment to allow for potential regrowth, this time
at day 138 when most of the remaining tumors had reached the humane endpoint. Despite cessation of
therapy, tumors showed no evidence of regrowth during the additional 100-day monitoring period (Figure
8G-H).

BH3 profiling of tumors collected at the humane endpoint that were treated with paclitaxel exhibited
increased apoptotic priming (Figure S8A) and BCL-XL dependence (Figure S8B), which supported the
increased sensitivity of the cells to DT2216. In addition, immunoblot analysis of DT2216 treated tumors
uncovered an increase in expression of MCL-1 as well as BCL-2 (Figure 8I), suggesting that RAA was
occurring in response to DT2216 and driving resistance despite clearly evident reduction in expression of
BCL-XL. The upregulation of both BCL-2 and MCL-1 in these tumors is likely responsible for the lack of
increased priming or dependence on BCL-XL or MCL-1 in tumors treated with only DT2216 as measured
by BH3 profiling at time of euthanasia (Figure S8A-B).

Toxicity assessment showed that these therapies were well tolerated, with no loss of body weight and
only transient reduction in platelet counts (Figure 8J-K). Pathological examination of tissue samples from
mice treated with vehicle, DT2216, paclitaxel, or a combination of both drugs revealed no detectable
signs of drug toxicity in the spleen, lung, liver, kidney, or heart (Figure S8C). To gain further insight into
the resiliency of platelets to DT2216 treatment, we BH3 profiled platelets in mice each day between
treatments with DT2216. Interestingly, we detected reduced priming in platelets 1 and 2 days after dosing
DT2216 as compared with vehicle-treated mice (Figure 8M) and also reduced dependence on BCL-XL
(Figure 8N). Interestingly, platelet dependence on MCL-1 was found to be elevated during this same
period (Figure 8N). Over the course of the next three days, these measures returned to baseline levels
comparable to vehicle treated mice. Since previous studies found that BCL-XL inhibitor treatment leads to
platelet loss and a compensatory increase in platelet production from megakaryocytes(80), these results
are consistent with platelets recently released into circulation being less primed for apoptosis and less
dependent on BCL-XL but more dependent on MCL-1. We also detected increased MCL-1 dependence in
PBMCs collected from mice treated with this DT2216 time course (Figure S8D-E), indicating that
degradation of BCL-XL is also occurring in PBMCs and that this may be a potential pharmacodynamic
biomarker for on-target activity of this agent.

In a separate cohort of DF83 PDX-bearing mice, we performed tumor cell analysis after short term (48
hour) treatment to examine drug-induced effects. Annexin V staining showed an enhancement of
apoptosis induction in EPCAM+ DF83 tumor cells in combination treated mice as compared to single
agents (Figure 8L). We also detected on-target degradation of BCL-XL upon DT2216 treatment and
decreased MCL-1 expression with paclitaxel treatment (Figure 8O), as well as increased MCL-1
dependence in PDX tumors treated with DT2216, confirming on-target activity and shifting apoptotic
dependencies under treatment pressure in vivo (Figure S8F-G).

Furthermore, formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) sections of the tumors collected after short term
treatment were also analyzed via immunofluorescence staining, which showed that BCL-XL expression is
increased in DF83 PDX tumor cells by paclitaxel treatment (Figure S8H). Consistent with the western
blotting data, DT2216 treatment alone or in combination with paclitaxel reduced BCL-XL expression
levels in EPCAM+ ovarian tumor cells (Figure S8H). Cleaved caspase 3 staining, indicating activation of
this executioner caspase, showed that the combination treatment resulted in extensive apoptosis of ovarian
tumor cells (Figure S8I). Taken together, these data indicate that combination treatment is most effective
at inducing the changes in BCL-2 family protein expression and interactions that are required for
meaningful HGSOC tumor regressions.

Finally, we evaluated the reduced-intensity (weekly paclitaxel) therapy in a model of disseminated
HGSOC disease by establishing luciferized DF83 PDX cells in the peritoneal cavities of
immunocompromised mice, which more closely matches the common growth of HGSOC as ascites in
women(81). When tumor bioluminescence reached 105 photons, we initiated therapy and monitored
responses. Single agent DT2216 or paclitaxel were both able to reduce tumor burden and extend median
survival, yet the combination therapy produced faster and more durable regressions of tumors (Figure 8P-
S). This treatment was again well tolerated despite continued treatment with DT2216 until humane
endpoint (disease burden equivalent to 108 photons) was reached (Figure 8T, S8J). Importantly, tumors
treated with combination therapy exhibited bioluminescent signals at or below background levels at day
40, which was not achieved with either single agent treatment (Figure 8Q-R). The deeper and more
durable regressions in combination treated animals led to a 35-day extension of median survival versus
paclitaxel alone (Figure 8S) and demonstrated the efficacy of this therapy even in the setting of
disseminated disease and high tumor burden at initiation of therapy.


DISCUSSION

Despite the increasing and well-founded interest in developing targeted therapies for a wide range of
malignancies, cytotoxic chemotherapies have been proven to be highly effective and eradicated cancers in
millions of patients, including some women with HGSOC(8). These chemotherapeutics, which include
the HGSOC first-line therapies carboplatin and paclitaxel, target ubiquitous cell features, including DNA
and microtubules, and typically kill cancer cells via the apoptosis pathway(17, 23, 82). This provides a
strong rationale for the identification of apoptosis-promoting agents that can be used in combination with
cytotoxic chemotherapies to enhance the chemosensitivity of cancer cells while maintaining a tolerable
safety profile. Indeed, herein we have reported that BCL-XL PROTAC degrader DT2216 can be safely
combined with paclitaxel to induce extensive cancer cell apoptosis in vitro and in vivo. In fact, this
combination consistently eradicates HGSOC cell line and PDX tumors in vivo – a striking result given the
inherent aggressiveness and chemoresistance of these models. Importantly, DT2216 induces only
transient and moderate thrombocytopenia and no other detectable signs of toxicity, which suggests that
this agent will be better tolerated by patients than conventional inhibitors of BCL-XL. The lack of
additional toxicity is consistent with our previous work showing that parenchymal cells within most adult
healthy tissues are resistant to apoptosis due to low expression of pro-apoptotic BAX and BAK(83),
rendering them resistant to BCL-XL inhibition(44). We believe this is the first clinically viable
combination strategy to effectively treat drug resistant OvCa by simultaneously targeting two of the most
important pro-survival proteins selectively in tumor cells but not in normal tissues, i.e. direct degradation
of BCL-XL with DT2216 and indirect inhibition of MCL-1 with paclitaxel-induced mitotic arrest. This
unique combination allows us to overcome the on-target toxicities caused by simultaneously targeting
BCL-XL and MCL-1 using direct small molecule inhibitors with or without conventional chemotherapy as
shown in many previous studies. These toxicities include BCL-XL inhibitor-induced dose-limiting
thrombocytopenia(36, 84) and MCL-1 inhibitor-induced cardiotoxicity(85). Further, combined direct
inhibition of BCL-XL and MCL-1 leads to severe and widespread tissue toxicity due to vascular
endothelial cell apoptosis due to their high apoptotic priming and dependence on these pro-survival
proteins for survival(44). These on-target and dose-limiting toxicities have stalled the clinical
development of these otherwise promising inhibitors. The lack of vascular toxicity in our in vivo models
is likely driven by the lack of active proliferation in vascular endothelial cells when angiogenesis is not
stimulated(86) – this protects these cells from paclitaxel-induced mitotic arrest and downregulation of
MCL-1. Our combination strategy avoids these toxicities and also produced a remarkable synergistic
induction of apoptosis in OvCa cells, resulting in complete eradications of tumors and cures in both
HGSOC cell line and patient-derived xenograft models without any detectable toxicities. These results are
particularly notable given the inherent therapy resistance of the heavily pretreated PDX models that we
have used for our studies(87). Our pre-clinical studies supported the initiation of a phase Ib study using
DT2216 plus weekly paclitaxel to treat relapsed/refractory ovarian cancer patients (clinicaltrials.gov ID
NCT06964009). We expect additional trials to be launched for other cancer types in the near future,
especially since paclitaxel is a standard of care agent in other solid cancers including breast and non-small
cell lung cancer (NSCLC)(88).

Our studies demonstrate the existence of endogenous and therapy-enhanced dependence of ovarian
cancers on the pro-survival protein BCL-XL and provide critical guidance for targeting this protein safely
and effectively using the DT2216 PROTAC. We show, for the first time, that HGSOC cells undergo RAA
in response to treatment with BH3 mimetics targeting BCL-XL, resulting in higher expression of
resistance-promoting BCL-2 or MCL-1. This is consistent with the selection for overexpression of
untargeted pro-survival proteins reported with BH3 mimetics in other cancer types(89, 90). Importantly,
this adaptation also involved upregulation of BCL-XL in response to ABT-263 but not DT2216 – this
demonstrates that proteasome-mediated degradation of BCL-XL with DT2216 may reduce the likelihood
of therapy resistance arising compared with ABT-263. It is important to note that across multiple
experimental models, DT2216 proved to be more effective at inducing apoptosis than ABT-263.
Nevertheless, the apoptotic adaptation in HGSOC suggests that single-agent treatment with BCL-XL
targeting agents including DT2216 is unlikely to lead to durable responses in patients with HGSOC and
likely other solid cancer types. We found that paclitaxel helped prevent apoptotic adaptation via several
different mechanisms, each leading to a sensitization to DT2216. First, paclitaxel treatment led to
decreased MCL-1 levels, as was evident in the OVCAR3 and OVCAR4 cell lines as well as tumors and
DF83 PDX model. This was especially evident in cells that are arrested in mitosis – these cells exhibit a
near-complete absence of MCL-1 expression and are particularly vulnerable to BCL-XL inhibition. This
decrease in MCL-1 within mitotically arrested cells likely forestalled the development of resistance to
DT2216, leading to the enhanced responses we observed, and is consistent with previous reports showing
MCL-1 phosphorylation and degradation during prolonged mitotic arrest(91–93). Second, paclitaxel
treatment also resulted in phosphorylation of BCL-XL across most HGSOC cells, as evidenced by the
appearance of the higher molecular weight band in the western blots and confirmed with the
phosphorylation-specific antibody. This phosphorylation of BCL-XL at Ser-62 has been previously
reported to prevent BCL-XL binding to BAX(67), which would result in a further reduction of anti-
apoptotic activity of total BCL-XL. There was no observed difference in DT2216-induced degradation of
phosphorylated versus non-phosphorylated BCL-XL. Our immunoprecipitation analysis confirmed that
paclitaxel and DT2216 treatment frees pro-apoptotic proteins BIM, BAX and BAK from being bound by
BCL-XL, enabling rapid and complete commitment to apoptotic cell death. Finally, paclitaxel also
downregulated HSP27, which would further facilitate the commitment to apoptosis. In vitro, these effects
led to synergistic induction of apoptosis in HGSOC cells. In vivo, these effects enhanced tumor control in
the combination treated mice compared with single agents, leading to the eradication of xenograft tumors
in the recurrent and chemoresistant HGSOC DF83 PDX model as well as other xenograft models. As
mentioned, our results provided the preclinical evidence for the initiation of a clinical trial evaluating
DT2216 in combination with paclitaxel for patients with recurrent HGSOC. This trial will also test the
extent to which BH3 profiling can predict patient outcomes since we demonstrated that BCL-XL
dependence can be reliably detected with this assay. Given the rising interest in using functional precision
medicine to guide treatment decisions(94, 95), our results are expected to inform the use of these
technologies in the clinic.

We have previously reported that HGSOC tumors are sensitive to pro-apoptotic stimuli(23), helping to set
the stage for successful deployment of DT2216 in combination with paclitaxel in this disease. It is
important to note that the near-ubiquitous nature of TP53 mutations in HGSOC impair the chemo- and
radio-sensitivity of these cancers but because BH3 mimetics engage the apoptosis pathway downstream
of p53 signaling, these agents can maintain high levels of efficacy despite lack of p53 signaling. Indeed,
we found that a diverse array of primary tumors, PDX and PDO models were sensitive to BCL-XL
inhibition, suggesting that the activity of this agent will be broad, including in treatment-naïve as well as
heavily pretreated patients. Also, since many of the targeted anti-cancer agents currently in development
will also produce pro-apoptotic signaling in OvCa cells, we expect BH3 mimetics targeting BCL-XL to
have an enduring impact on the clinical care of these patients. Finally, although our validation in vitro and
in vivo studies focused on HGSOC, we expect DT2216 to be highly effective in other subtypes of OvCa
and the many other solid cancer types that we and others have identified as BCL-XL dependent including
cancers of the lung(96), pancreas(97), colon(98), breast(99), kidney(100) and others(101). Importantly,
our data indicate that targeting BCL-XL will be effective even in tumors with mutant TP53, which are
resistant to many other classes of anti-cancer agents that induce DNA damage and rely on intact p53
signaling to trigger apoptosis.

Our comprehensive pan-cancer analysis of BCL-2 family dependencies provides critical insights into the
therapeutic potential of targeting the apoptosis pathway across diverse malignancies. By analyzing nearly
2,000 cancer cell lines from publicly available databases and validating findings through direct apoptosis
measurements in 132 cell lines, we clarify both the opportunities and limitations inherent in apoptotic
targeting strategies. The striking finding that simultaneous BCL-XL and MCL-1 inhibition induced
synergistic apoptosis in 88.6% of cancer cell lines, including notoriously chemoresistant types such as
glioblastoma, pancreatic, and ovarian cancers, demonstrates the broad therapeutic potential of dual
apoptotic targeting. Importantly, our validation studies using Annexin V staining to directly measure
apoptosis, rather than inferring cell death from reduced representation of barcoded cell lines in pooled
screens, provides definitive evidence that these combination effects represent true apoptotic cell death
rather than cytostatic effects that can potentially complicate interpretation of large-scale pooled screens.
Our data further extend this principle beyond direct MCL-1/BCL-XL co-inhibition by showing that
DT2216 and BCL-XL inhibition more broadly synergistically enhance apoptosis induced by multiple
mitotic arrest–inducing therapies, including Aurora A kinase inhibitors, PLK1 inhibitors, and
microtubule-targeting agents, many of which are commonly used in the clinic for a wide range of
malignancies. This also has important translational relevance for ADCs, which represent a rapidly
expanding therapeutic class with 15 FDA-approved agents and over 50 in late-stage clinical development
(68) – most of these deliver microtubule-disrupting payloads that we found induce apoptosis
synergistically when combined with BCL-XL inhibition. Across these clinically relevant modalities,
apoptotic resistance represents a shared mechanism of chemoresistance, and our findings suggest that
BCL-XL targeting with agents such as DT2216 can safely lower the apoptotic threshold to potentiate
mitotic arrest–induced tumor cell killing.

Our analysis also uncovers why single-agent BH3 mimetics have shown limited clinical success in solid
tumors despite their efficacy in hematologic malignancies. The functional redundancy between BCL-XL
and MCL-1, evidenced by their co-expression patterns and compensatory upregulation following single-
agent treatment, explains the modest single-agent activities observed across most solid cancer types. This
compensatory mechanism, which we term "rapid apoptotic adaptation," represents a fundamental barrier
to apoptotic targeting that has been underappreciated in previous studies. Our identification of HSP27 as a
key mediator of rapid apoptotic adaptation provides important mechanistic insights with broad therapeutic
implications beyond solid tumors. HSP27 phosphorylation at Ser82 in response to BCL-XL inhibition
represents a previously unrecognized resistance mechanism that enables cancer cells to rapidly upregulate
compensatory pro-survival proteins, thereby blunting the apoptotic response to BH3 mimetics. The ability
of the HSP27 inhibitor J2(58) to prevent this adaptive response and enhance apoptosis induced by both
ABT-263 and DT2216 suggests that targeting this chaperone protein could significantly improve the
efficacy of single-agent BH3 mimetics. This finding may be particularly relevant for hematologic
malignancies such as chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and acute myeloid leukemia (AML), where
venetoclax has shown remarkable initial responses but acquired resistance frequently develops through
upregulation of alternative pro-survival proteins like MCL-1 and BCL-XL(102). Co-targeting HSP27
alongside established BH3 mimetics could potentially prevent or delay the emergence of resistance in
these diseases.

Our BH3 profiling analysis of platelets after treatment with DT2216 provides novel insights into the
regulation of apoptosis in these cells in a preclinical therapeutic setting. Specifically, we found that
dosing mice with DT2216 led to decreased apoptotic priming and decreased, but still detectable, BCL-XL
dependence in circulating platelets one and two days after treatment, returning to baseline (equivalent to
vehicle-treated animals) on the third day. These data, combined with our detection of short-term decreases
in platelet counts after DT2216 administration, suggest that newly-produced platelets that are recently
released into circulation are more resistant to apoptosis. This can be due to lower or higher expression of
pro-apoptotic or pro-survival proteins, respectively. We also detected dependence on MCL-1 in these
newly-formed platelets, which was not detected in vehicle-treated animals. Taken together, these data
suggest that MCL-1 expression is higher in newly-formed platelets, which protects them from BCL-XL
targeted therapies to some extent. Given that megakaryocytes in the bone marrow express both BCL-XL
and MCL-1(103), the mitochondria-containing platelets they shed into circulation likely contain high
expression levels of both these proteins. However, due to the short half-life of MCL-1(104), this protein is
expected to be rapidly lost in circulating platelets, resulting in sole dependence on BCL-XL. Thus, the
ability of DT2216 therapy to avoid chronic thrombocytopenia is predicated upon both the lower VHL
expression of platelets and also the dosing schedule of this agent, which maintains degradation and thus
inhibition of BCL-XL in tumor cells with twice weekly administration while allowing apoptosis-resistant,
MCL-1 expressing, newly formed platelets to be released into circulation. These findings also help
explain why lead-in dosing with ABT-263 is able to reduce the severity of thrombocytopenia to some
extent in patients, but not enough to avoid thrombocytopenia that can limit dosing(36). Based on our data,
we expect DT2216 treated patients to potentially experience transient thrombocytopenia that is then
stabilized after new platelet production is enhanced. Interestingly, thrombopoietin agonists such as
Eltrombopag or romiplostim that stimulate production of new, apoptosis-resistant platelets(105) may help
prevent even transient thrombocytopenia in DT2216 treated patients.

Although our findings provide a strong mechanistic and translational rationale for combining paclitaxel
with DT2216, several limitations should be considered. First, while the combination produced striking
tumor regressions and eradications in HGSOC cell line xenografts and PDX models, these studies remain
preclinical and may not fully recapitulate the complexity, heterogeneity, pharmacokinetics, and treatment
history of human cancer. In addition, our mechanistic studies identify paclitaxel-induced mitotic arrest,
MCL-1 loss, BCL-XL dependence, and suppression of rapid apoptotic adaptation as key contributors to
synergy, but additional determinants of response and resistance are likely to emerge, including potential
tumor-specific differences in mitotic arrest duration, apoptotic priming, drug efflux, and expression of
other pro-survival proteins. Finally, although our pan-cancer analyses suggest broad BCL-XL/MCL-1 co-
dependence across solid tumors, our most extensive functional and in vivo validation was performed in
HGSOC models; the efficacy and optimal combination partners for DT2216 in other cancer types remain
to be established. Thus, ongoing and future clinical studies will be essential to define the safety,
tolerability, biomarkers, and therapeutic activity of this strategy in patients.

Overall, our findings have important clinical implications and suggest that targeting single pro-survival
proteins for solid cancer therapy will not be sufficient. Instead, indirectly targeting MCL-1 with mitotic-
arrest inducing agents such as paclitaxel in combination with DT2216-mediated BCL-XL degradation is a
novel therapeutic strategy that is immediately clinically actionable and has the potential to be broadly
effective across solid caners while also circumventing the vascular toxicity that prohibits direct dual
inhibition of both BCL-XL and MCL-1. This work establishes a framework for rational combination
design in apoptotic targeting and suggests that the true therapeutic potential of this pathway lies in
carefully designed direct and indirect combination strategies that can simultaneously target the BCL-XL-
MCL-1 axis rather than single-agent approaches.

Cell and organoid culture

10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin. Human and mouse platelets were kept in Hank’s Balanced Salt

in accordance with a Dana-Farber/Harvard Cancer Center Institutional Review Board-approved protocol,
with written informed consent obtained from all participants.

BH3 profiling

BH3 profiling was performed using flow cytometry according to established protocols(48, 107). Briefly,
cultured cells were treated with trypsin (Gibco), collected and centrifuged at 200 g for 5 minutes. The cell
pellet was resuspended in Mannitol Experimental Buffer (MEB), which consists of 10 mM HEPES (pH
7.5), 150 mM mannitol, 50 mM KCl, 0.02 mM EGTA, 0.02 mM EDTA, 0.1% BSA, and 5 mM succinate.
The resuspended cells were subsequently added to wells of prepared plates containing the indicated
peptide conditions and 0.001% digitonin. Alamethicin (25 μM) was used as positive controls, while
DMSO was used as negative controls. After incubation for 60 minutes at 28°C, cells were fixed with 8%
paraformaldehyde for 15 minutes, and then neutralized with N2 buffer (1.7 M Tris base, 1.25 M glycine,
pH 9.1). Cells were stained overnight with DAPI (1:1000, Abcam) and anti-Cytochrome c-Alexa Fluor
647 (1:2000, clone 6H2.B4, Biolegend) in a Tween20-based intracellular stain buffer (0.2% Tween20, 1%
BSA). Cytochrome c release in response to BH3 peptide treatment was measured using an Attune NxT
flow cytometer equipped with an autosampler (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

In vitro drug treatments and Annexin V (AxV) / Propidium iodide (PI) viability assay

Cells were initially plated at a density of 104 cells per well in 100 μL of culture media in flat-bottom 96-
well plates (Denville). Following plating, cells were treated with various drugs at the specified
concentrations: ABT-199 (1, 0.1, 0.01 μM), ABT-263 (1, 0.1, 0.01 μM), A-1331852 (1, 0.1, 0.01 μM),
S63845 (1, 0.1, 0.01 μM), DT2216 (1, 0.1, 0.01 μM), ABT-737 (1, 0.1, 0.01 μM), Carboplatin (25, 100,
250 μM), Paclitaxel (0.3, 1, 3, 10, 30, 100, 300 nM), and QVD-OPH (10 μM). Combination treatments
were administered simultaneously. DMSO (1%) was used as negative control. After 24, 48 or 72 hours of

96-well V-bottom plate (Sigma Aldrich). 25 μL of 0.0025% Trypsin (Gibco) was added to each well on
the original plate and incubated for 5 minutes, trypsinized cells were then transferred to the V-bottom

(Abcam) at a dilution of 1:500. The staining solution was added to the cells at a final dilution of 1:10 and
incubated for 30 minutes on ice in the dark. AxV/PI positivity was then measured using an Attune NxT
flow cytometer equipped with an autosampler (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

siRNA transfection

medium and transfected the next day with BCL2L1 siRNA (Horizon Discovery, L-003458-00-0010) or a

cells per well in 100 μL of culture medium, treated the next day with the indicated drugs at specified
concentrations, and assessed for viability 72 h later using Annexin V/propidium iodide (Annexin V/PI)
staining.

Organoid CTG synergy assay

DT2216 and paclitaxel (PTX) for one week. The media containing the drugs was refreshed every other
day. PTX concentrations ranged from 0 to 30 nM (0, 1.5, 3, 10, 30 nM), while DT2216 concentrations
ranged from 0 to 1000 nM (0, 10, 30, 100, 300, 1000 nM). Synergy between the two drugs was evaluated
using the SynergyFinder web application (https://synergyfinder.fimm.fi/) with calculations based on the
Zero-interaction model, where a synergy scores above 10 indicates a synergistic interaction. For the
apoptosis assay, 45,000 cells were seeded per well in a 48-well plate in a 15 μL Matrigel dome and
similarly incubated with PTX at 1.5 nM and DT2216 at 10 nM for one week, with media changes every
other day. Apoptosis was then assessed using FITC-Annexin V and 7-AAD by flow cytometry.

with 5% CO₂, cells were treated with paclitaxel at 100 nM. The plate was then transferred to the Cellcyte
live-cell imaging system (Discover Echo), and time-lapse images and videos were captured using red and
green fluorescence channels to visualize mitochondrial membrane potential changes and caspase-3/7
activation, respectively.

Mitotic shake-off

OVCAR3 cells were plated in a T175 cm² flask with 5.0 × 10⁶ cells per flask. The following day, the cells
were treated with DMSO or paclitaxel (20 nM) for 24 hours to induce mitotic arrest. After the incubation,
the medium was gently aspirated, and the cells were washed once with PBS to remove residual paclitaxel
and serum. Fresh RPMI medium was added, and the flask was placed on a shaker set to 300 rpm for 5
minutes to collect loosely attached cells (shake-off). Detached mitotic cells were collected into a clean
centrifuge tube. The attached cells were then detached by adding trypsin. After neutralizing the trypsin
with RPMI medium containing serum, the non-mitotic cells were collected. Both mitotic and non-mitotic
cells were counted using a hemocytometer or an automated cell counter. The mitotic and non-mitotic
fractions were then used for BH3 profiling and Western blot analysis as required.

Protein immunoprecipitation

Cells were seeded in 6-well plates and treated with drugs when they reached approximately 70%
confluency. After treatment, the cells were harvested by washing twice with cold PBS and then lysed on
ice using in Tris Lysis Buffer (R60TX-3) containing a Protease Inhibitor Cocktail (Meso Scale
Diagnostics, R70AA-1). The lysates were centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected. Protein
concentration was determined using the Pierce™ Microplate BCA Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Fisher
Scientific). Based on the protein concentration, 200-600 μg of protein was aliquoted and adjusted to a
final volume of 250 μL with Tris Lysis Buffer for “Input” group. For immunoprecipitation, 200-600 μg of
protein was combined with Tris Lysis Buffer to a total volume of 120 μL, followed by the addition of 50
μL of diluted antibody and 50 μL of Pierce Protein A/G Magnetic Beads (Thermo Fisher). Mouse anti-
human BCL-XL antibodies (EMD Millipore MAB3121) were used for BCL-XL immunoprecipitation, and
Mouse anti-human MCL-1 antibodies (BD Pharmingen 559027) were used for MCL-1
immunoprecipitation. The mixtures were incubated overnight on a rocker in the cold room to allow
binding. Following incubation, the immunoprecipitated complexes were separated using magnetic
separation, and the beads were washed three times with Tris Lysis Buffer on ice. Proteins were eluted by
heating the samples at 95°C for 10 minutes in Tris Lysis Buffer with LDS Sample Buffer 4X (Invitrogen).
A representative aliquot of the normalized whole-cell lysate was saved for Western blot analysis.

Mouse xenograft studies

For the flank xenograft models, cell suspensions of OVCAR3, OVCAR8, or DF83 cells were prepared in
a 1:1 mixture of Matrigel and PBS. A total of 1 × 106 cells were injected subcutaneously into the flanks of
8-16 week old female immunodeficient NOD-scid IL2Rgammanull (NSG) mice (Jackson Laboratories
strain #005557) and allowed to grow to approximately 65-100 mm3, at which point treatment was
commenced (on average, the time period before initiation of treatment was 14 days for the OVCAR3, 18
days for OVCAR8, and 17 days for DF83 flank xenograft models). For the intraperitoneal injection (IP)
xenograft models, luciferase expressing DF83 (DF83-Luc) cells were injected into the intraperitoneal
cavities of 8-16 week old female NSG mice. When tumor bioluminescence reached 105 photons, we
initiated therapy and monitored responses (on average, the time period before initiation of treatment was
24 days for this DF83 intraperitoneal model). DF83-Luc tumor bearing mice were imaged at post-
implantation weekly by intraperitoneal injection with D-luciferin (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 50-209-
8110) and were euthanized when luminescence signals reached 108 photons or mice became moribund in
accordance with IACUC regulations. Mice in each xenograft model were then randomly assigned to
different treatment groups. The paclitaxel cohort received intraperitoneal injection of 15 mg/kg every four
days for four doses. The DT2216 cohort received intraperitoneal injection of 15 mg/kg every four days.
The combination cohort received both paclitaxel and DT2216, while the vehicle cohort received
corresponding vehicles. Tumor growth and body weight were monitored every other day using electronic
calipers, and tumor volume was calculated using the formula for volume of an ellipsoid (V = 4/3 × π ×
Length/2 × (Width/2)2). Blood samples were collected via tail vein every four days or every week in
different xenograft models, and whole blood cell counts were performed using the Hemavet 950 analyzer
(Drew Scientific). Mice were euthanized when tumors reached the predetermined endpoint of a volume of
1,767 mm3 (equivalent to an ellipsoid volume reflecting a xenograft of 15 mm length and width). For
each treatment, single-animal and average tumor sizes are represented in charts and averages included
tumor size reached at humane endpoint in subsequent average calculations. Tumors and healthy tissues
were collected for further analysis. Healthy tissues were rinsed with Hank's Balanced Salt Solution and
placed into 4% paraformaldehyde (Electron Microscopy Sciences) for 24 hours. Sections were then
stained with hematoxylin and eosin stain, and microphotographs of the sections were captured using a
Leica DM2000 microscope equipped with a 40X objective.

Human CA125/MUC16 ELISA analysis

The blood from DF83-Luc IP xenograft models were collected and centrifuged at 1000 × g for 15
minutes, and the resulting serum was collected and stored for CA125/MUC16 ELISA analysis. Serum
CA125/MUC16 levels were quantified using a Human CA125/MUC16 ELISA kit (R&D Systems,)
according to the manufacturer’s protocol. Serum samples were diluted 1:24 using the provided calibrator
diluent and added in duplicate to the wells of a supplied plate. Plates were incubated for 2 hours at room
temperature on a microplate shaker, followed by washes and the addition of Human CA125 Conjugate.
After 2-hour incubation at room temperature on a microplate shaker, wells were washed again and
incubated with substrate solution for 30 minutes, protected from light. The reaction was stopped by
adding the provided stop solution, and absorbance was measured at 450 nm with 540 nm correction using
a SpectraMax iD3 Microplate Reader. CA125 concentrations were calculated by generating a standard
curve based on the supplied standards.

BH3 profiling of patient tumor specimens and murine xenograft tumors

Tissue samples were initially minced into approximately 1mm3 pieces using scalpels and then subjected to
enzymatic dissociation with collagenase/dispase for 30 minutes at 37°C. The resulting cell suspensions
were then filtered through a 100 mm filter to obtain single-cell preparations, which were then centrifuged
at 200g for 5 minutes at 4°C to pellet the cells. The cell pellet was resuspended and cultured in
RPMI1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin for 24 hours in the absence or
presence of the specified drugs. Following culture, cells were collected and stained with specific
antibodies: anti-human EpCAM for primary patient and xenograft samples, anti-human or anti-mouse
CD61 for platelets, and anti-human or anti-mouse CD45 for PBMCs. Subsequently, Flow cytometry
based BH3 profiling was then performed. PBMCs were isolated using anti-human CD45 staining in
primary patient and anti-mouse CD45 for xenograft samples, and the remaining cell identity stains were
analyzed on CD45 negative-gated cells.

Paraffin-embedding of tissues
NSG mice bearing subcutaneous DF83 PDX tumors were treated with either vehicle, paclitaxel, DT2216
or paclitaxel + DT2216 and tumors were collected 48 hours later and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde
(Electron Microscopy Sciences) diluted in 1X PBS (Boston Bioproducts Inc.) overnight, or longer, then
transferred to 70% ethanol for ≥1 hour and stored at 4 °C. Samples were dehydrated through graded
ethanol (95% for 45 min and 30 min; 100% for 45 min and 30 min), cleared in xylene (45 min, 1 h,
30 min), and paraffin-infiltrated at 60 °C for 3 × 1 h. Embedding was performed using standard
procedures. Reagents: VWR; Polyfin (19280-01); Superfrost Plus slides (48311-703).

Immunofluorescence staining of xenograft tumors

Sections were first warmed to 60°C in a vacuum incubator (Isotemp Vacuum Oven, Thermo Fisher
Scientific) overnight and then washed twice in Histo-Clear (Electron Microscopy Sciences), and
gradually redehydrated in ethanol (100%, 100%, 70%, 50%) followed by 1X PBS. Antigen retrieval was
performed with 1X eBioscience™ IHC Antigen Retrieval Solution-High pH containing 10 mM Tris, 1
mM EDTA solution, pH 9.0 (Invitrogen). Sections were placed in a 3% bleach solution [4 ml of 30%
H2O2 (Sigma-Aldrich) and 0.8 ml of 1 M NaOH (Sigma-Aldrich) in 35.2 ml of PBS (Boston Bioproducts
Inc.)], sandwiched between two 15,000 lux lights (AMZCOOL), and subjected to photobleaching for 1
hour. Sections were washed three times with 1X PBS for 5 minutes each and permeabilized in 0.5% PBS-
T (Triton X-100–based, CST) for 15 minutes at room temperature twice. After washing with 1X PBS
three times for 5 minutes each, sections were blocked with blocking buffer [10% goat serum (Gibco), 2%
Bovine Serum Albumin (BSA-Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories ,Inc.), 0.1% Triton-X-100 (CST)
in 1X Tris Buffered Saline with Tween 20 (CST)] for 45 minutes. Primary antibody against BCL-
XL (CST, 2764, 1:100), and Cleaved Caspase 3 (CST, 9661, 1:400) were diluted in a blocking buffer with
an overnight incubation at 4°C. Fluorophore-conjugated, species-specific secondary antibody conjugated
to either Alexa Fluor 488 (Invitrogen, A11032) or Alexa Fluor 568 (Invitrogen, A11036) was incubated
for 1 hour at room temperature at 1:1000 dilution in the blocking buffer. Sections were then incubated
with primary antibody targeting human EpCAM conjugated with Alexa Fluor 647 (SantaCruz, sc-25308
AF647) at 1:100 dilution and DAPI at 1:200 dilution for 1 hour at room temperature. Sections were
mounted with SlowFade™ Glass Soft-Set Antifade Mountant (Invitrogen, S36917). Images were
captured with a Zeiss Axio-Observer Z1 inverted fluorescent microscope equipped with an Apotome.2
structured illumination optical sectioning device and an Axiocam 712 color camera. One animal per
treatment condition was used, and three 10× images were captured per animal (i.e., 3 images total per
treatment condition).

Isolation of PBMCs and platelets

Peripheral blood was collected from human donors in compliance with an IRB-approved protocol at
Harvard University, using lavender-top tubes containing EDTA. The peripheral blood was diluted with an
equal volume phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) and then carefully layered over Ficoll-Paque (GE
Healthcare). After centrifugation at 177 g for 20 minutes at room temperature with low brake settings, the
supernatant enriched with platelets was transferred to a new tube. Prostaglandin E1 (PGE1) was added to
achieve a final dilution of 1:10,000 from a 10mM stock solution. The solution was then centrifuged at
1000 g for 5 minutes at room temperature. The resulting pellet, which contained platelets, was
resuspended with HBSS and counted. The interface layer, containing PBMCs interface, was carefully
aspirated and washed with PBS before counting. The isolated PBMCs and platelets were subsequently
used for BH3 profiling and chemosensitivity assay.

Colony formation assay

OVCAR3, OVCAR8, KURAMOCHI, and OVKATE cells were seeded 600 cells per well into 24-well
plates. Cells were then treated with the indicated conditions the following day. After 24 hours of
exposure, the medium was replaced with fresh medium, and the cells were incubated for additional two
weeks under standard culture conditions. At the end of the incubation period, the medium was removed,
and the cells were washed with PBS. The cells were subsequently stained with 0.05% crystal violet
solution for 30 minutes. Following staining, the crystal violet solution was removed, and the cells were
washed twice with PBS. Stained colonies were imaged using the ECL Detection System (Thermo Fisher
Scientific, 32209).

ProteinAtlas.org

PDX models

With the exception of DF09 and DF20, which were derived from therapy-naïve patients, the primary
ovarian tumors used for the generation of the PDX models were isolated from women with advanced high
grade serous ovarian cancer that have been treated with multiple chemotherapies. The establishment of
these models, their clinical and genomic characteristics (e.g., BRCA mutations) have been previously
described(76). mCherry–Luciferase was transduced into dissociated tumor cells in vitro with a lentiviral
vector and then placed under puromycin selection for 5–7 days before expansion in vivo (up to six
passages) to generate viably frozen stocks. PDX models tested negative for mycoplasma contamination
and were authenticated by genomic comparison with the original tumor.

PDX ex vivo dose-response analyses

Frozen PDX ascites cells were thawed and allowed to recover for two days in a 1:1:1 ratio of Medium
199 (11150067, Life Tech), RPMI-1640 (11875119, Thermo Fisher) and DMEM/F12 (11330057,
Thermo Fisher), supplemented with 2% HI-FBS (Corning), 1% (v/v) Penicillin-Streptomycin (Sigma-
Aldrich), 1% (v/v) Insulin-Transferrin-Selenium (354352, Corning), 0.5 ng/ml of 17 beta-estradiol (US
Biological), 0.2 pg/mL of triiodothyronine (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.025 ug/mL all-trans retinoic acid
(Beantown Chemical), 20 ug/mL of insulin (Sigma-Aldrich), 0.5 ng/mL of EGF (Peprotech), 0.5 mg/mL
hydrocortisone (Sigma-Aldrich) and 25 ng/mL of Cholera Toxin (Calbiochem). Cells were then seeded
into 384-well white plates (3570, Corning) in a 1:1 ratio of MCDB105 (117-500, Westnet), M199
medium (11150067, Life Tech) supplemented with 2% HI-FBS and 1% (v/v) Penicillin-Streptomycin.
After 4 hours of recovery in the incubator, cells in 384 well-plates were treated with indicated dose ranges
of DT2216 and paclitaxel using a HP D300 drug printer. Dose combination studies were performed with a
dose of DT2216 starting from 10 μM followed by log10 based serial dilution for nine dose ranges (0.05 -
10 μM), and the same log10 based serial dilution for paclitaxel (0.05 - 10 μM) for five dose ranges.
DMSO was used as vehicle control. After 96 hours of drug treatment, cell viability was measured by
luciferase assay with a PerkinElmer Envision plate reader (384-L1 aperture). Briefly, 20 μL of 1mg/mL
D-Luciferin (LUCK-1G, GoldBio) in DPBS was added to each well of the 384-well plates, and then
plates were shaken for 1 minute and incubated for 1 minute before reading. To determine growth rate, a
parallel plate was read on the day of drug addition to calculate the luciferase signal before treatment (Day
0).

Anchor combination screens of breast, colon and pancreas cancer cell lines analysis

Drug screening on cancer cell lines was performed by the Wellcome Sanger Institute as previously
described(109). Briefly, cell lines from commercial sources were authenticated by SNP and STR
profiling and cultured in appropriate media with routine mycoplasma testing. High-throughput
combination screening involved optimized seeding of cell lines in 1,536-well plates, compound dosing,
and viability quantification using CellTiter-Glo (Promega). Dose–response curves were fit with a two-
parameter sigmoid model and synergy was assessed using Bliss independence scoring with shifts in IC50
and Emax used to define synergistic interactions according to established thresholds.
